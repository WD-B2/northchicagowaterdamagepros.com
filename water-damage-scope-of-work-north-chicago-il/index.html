Water Damage Scope of Work in North Chicago: What It Should Include

A scope of work is the written plan for a water damage job. When it is clear, estimates are easier to compare, claims move faster and surprises are rare.

Published by North Chicago Water Damage Pros · Updated November 2026
24/7 Emergency Dispatch(847) 368-7353☎ Call Now

What a Scope of Work Is For

A scope of work describes what will be done, where, with what equipment and in what order. It protects you by stating the plan in writing, and it protects the company by showing what you agreed to.

For insurance, a good scope shows the adjuster why each step was needed. For repairs, it tells the contractor what condition the building is in when the drying crew leaves.

The Core Sections of a Good Scope

SectionWhat it should say
Property and loss detailsAddress, date of loss, source of water, water category
Affected areasRooms, floors and walls, with approximate measurements
Moisture readingsStarting readings in each material and the dry goal
Materials to removeWhich drywall, pad, insulation and flooring are being removed and why
Drying planEquipment types and counts, and planned monitoring visits
Cleaning and treatmentSanitizing, antimicrobial steps or containment if needed
ContentsWhat is moved, cleaned, stored or disposed of
ExclusionsWhat is not included, such as rebuild or plumbing repair
Price and termsCost structure, payment terms and how changes are handled

If any of these sections are missing, ask for them. A missing exclusions section is a frequent source of disputes, since rebuild items are often assumed to be included.

How Moisture Readings Anchor the Scope

Readings tie the scope to measurements. A technician records readings in drywall, wood, concrete and carpet at the start, then at each visit. The final readings show materials reached the dry goal.

The reference for this practice is ANSI/IICRC S500. Readings also protect you from paying for equipment that stays longer than needed, because the log shows when materials were dry. Our structural drying page explains how the process works.

What Changes the Scope Mid-Job

Scopes sometimes change. Opening a wall may show more wet insulation, a technician may find a second leak or a delay may allow growth to start. A good company explains the change before acting on it and updates the document.

ChangeWhat should happen
More wet material foundTechnician explains why and updates the scope and price before proceeding
Mold found behind a wallWork pauses, the area is contained and the scope is revised. See mold remediation
Source not fully stoppedPlumber or roofer is called, and drying timing adjusts
Insurer asks for changesBoth scopes are compared and the differences documented

Reading a Scope: Warning Signs

Questions to ask if something looks off

  • ✓Are there line items with no measurements or explanations?
  • ✓Does the equipment count match the size of the wet area?
  • ✓Is there a plan for monitoring and a dry goal?
  • ✓Are exclusions stated, so you know what comes later?
  • ✓Does the scope mention the water category and why it was chosen?

Using the Scope With Your Insurer

Share the scope, photographs and moisture log with your adjuster. If your insurer's estimate differs, ask for the differences in writing and discuss them with the technician who wrote the scope.

The Illinois Department of Insurance advises keeping documentation, photographs and receipts. Your insurer decides coverage, and we do not promise approval. We explain what we found and why each step is needed.

Using the Scope With Your Contractor

When the drying crew finishes, the rebuild contractor should receive the final readings and the list of materials removed. That handoff prevents rebuilding over damp framing and lets the contractor start with a clear picture.

For common repair paths, see our pages on ceiling water damage and wall and drywall repair.

A Sample Scope for a Small Kitchen Leak

A sample shows how the sections fit. Imagine a dishwasher hose failure that wets a kitchen floor and the base of two cabinets. This is a generalized example of how a scope reads, not a record of a specific job.

SectionWhat the scope might say
Loss detailsSource is a failed dishwasher supply hose, clean water, discovered the same day
Affected areasKitchen floor and the bases of two adjoining cabinets
ReadingsSubfloor and cabinet base readings recorded, with a dry goal for each material
WorkExtract water, remove toe kicks, dry cavities and monitor each day
EquipmentA set number of air movers and one dehumidifier for several days
ExclusionsCabinet repair, flooring replacement and plumbing are excluded

Notice how each row ties to something measurable. When the readings reach the dry goal, the equipment leaves, and the exclusions tell you what still needs another contractor.

Keeping Your Copy Safe

Save the scope, the moisture log, photographs and any change notes together in one folder, digital or paper. Insurers, contractors and buyers may ask for them months later, and having them in one place makes the request simple. Our kitchen water damage page shows how this kind of job usually runs.

What Good Communication Looks Like

A scope is only as good as the conversation around it. Expect the technician to walk through the document, answer questions in plain language and tell you promptly when something changes. If a company cannot explain its own scope, treat that as a warning sign.

Safety Steps a Scope Should Mention

Do these first

  • ✓Electrical hazards and how power to wet areas is handled.
  • ✓Protective equipment and containment for contaminated water or mold.
  • ✓How occupants, children and pets are kept out of the work area.

Why Scopes Matter in North Chicago Properties

North Chicago includes single-family homes, apartments and commercial space, and each has a different mix of owners, tenants and managers. A clear written scope helps everyone involved know what was done and what is still left.

The 2020 census counted about 7,900 housing units in the city. We serve North Chicago and also respond in Winthrop Harbor and Fort Sheridan, and we give each job a written scope before work starts.
