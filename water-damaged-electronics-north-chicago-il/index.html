Water Damaged Electronics in North Chicago: What to Do First

What you do in the first few minutes decides whether a wet phone, laptop or TV has a chance. The most important step is also the one people skip: do not turn it on.

Published by North Chicago Water Damage Pros · Updated November 2026
24/7 Emergency Dispatch(847) 368-7353☎ Call Now

Step One: Do Not Power It On

Water and electricity cause damage when the device is on. Powering up a wet device can short circuits that would otherwise survive, so unplug it, switch it off if it is still on and do not try to see whether it works.

If water is near outlets or cords, do not touch them. Leave the area and shut off power only if the breaker is dry and reachable. Safety comes before saving a gadget.

Larger home electronics may also be involved in a flooded space, as covered on our flood damage cleanup page. Safety rules for wet outlets and cords still apply while you wait.

The Rice Myth and Better Ideas

Putting a phone in a bag of rice is a popular fix, but it does little to pull water from inside the device, and rice dust can get into ports. Better approaches are to leave the device in open, dry air, or place it with silica gel packets in a sealed container.

Do not use heat. A hair dryer or oven can damage components and push water deeper into the device. Patience is more helpful than speed.

Why Salt Water and Contaminated Water Are Worse

Clean tap water usually leaves fewer deposits than flood or sewage water. Dirty water leaves minerals and contaminants that corrode electronics even after they dry. A technician may open the device and clean the board, and results vary.

If a device sat in sewage or flood water, treat it as contaminated, wear gloves and expect lower odds. Our sewage backup cleanup page explains the safety steps for contaminated spaces.

Insurance and Electronics

Electronics are personal property, which may be covered under a homeowners or renters policy for certain water events. The Illinois Department of Insurance advises photographing damage and keeping receipts. Photograph each device, its model plate and the wet area, and keep the device until your insurer says otherwise.

Your insurer decides coverage, and limits often apply to electronics. Ask your agent before you throw anything out.

Protecting Electronics Before a Loss

Keep important electronics off basement floors, and use a surge protector, not a basement outlet near a floor drain. Back up phones and computers on a schedule, and keep spare chargers and cords away from places that flood.

Cables, Chargers and Surge Protectors

Cords, power strips and surge protectors that were wet should be thrown away, not dried and reused. Water can corrode connections inside, and a damaged strip can cause shocks or fires. Chargers and adapters are inexpensive to replace and not worth the risk.

Keep them out of the room until dry and discard any that sat in standing water. When you replace them, plug new strips into outlets that are not near a floor drain or the lowest part of the room.

Batteries and Power Tools

Lithium batteries that took on water can overheat. Do not charge a wet battery, and do not use one that looks swollen or smells odd. Store it outside the house in a non-flammable container and ask your local household hazardous waste program how to dispose of it.

When to Call a Repair Specialist

Repair shops can open a device, clean corrosion and test components, and success depends on the water type and how long it sat. Ask about diagnostic fees before you agree, and ask what the shop does if the device cannot be saved. For drives with irreplaceable data, a data recovery lab is the safest option.

Standards and Safety for Wet Rooms

The room where electronics got wet is dried under ANSI/IICRC S500, the standard for water damage restoration, but the devices themselves are outside that standard. Treat them as an electrical safety question first and a repair question second.

Electronics in Lake County Basements and Home Offices

Basements often hold TVs, routers, servers and game consoles, and they flood more than upper floors. With about 36 inches of precipitation a year in Lake County and a history of urban flooding, moving valuable electronics to higher levels is practical.
