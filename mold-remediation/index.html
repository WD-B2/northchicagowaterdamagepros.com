Mold Starts With Water, So the Plan Starts There Too

Mold grows where materials stay damp, usually after a leak, a flood or chronic humidity. Cleaning visible growth without fixing the moisture only buys a few weeks before it returns.

That is why our process starts with a question: where did the water come from? A leaking pipe, a roof flashing failure, an AC drain, a damp crawl space and a flooded basement each lead to different repairs, and the mold plan follows the moisture plan.

Why Does Mold Keep Coming Back in My Basement?

The usual answer is that the moisture never left. Scrubbing a stain with bleach or a household cleaner removes the visible color, but damp materials and humid air let it return within weeks.

A hidden leak is still active

A pipe, a drain line or a window well may keep feeding the wall even though the surface looks dry.

Humidity stays high

A basement with persistently high humidity supports growth on cool surfaces.

Porous materials stayed in place

Drywall, carpet and cardboard that stayed wet usually hold growth that cleaning cannot reach.

Drying stopped too soon

A wall that feels dry may still read wet inside, and a moisture meter shows the difference.

We do not install permanent waterproofing, and we will not promise that water will never return. If readings show an ongoing seepage problem, you will need grading, gutter, drainage or waterproofing work on top of the cleanup.

How Containment and Removal Work

A crew seals the work area with plastic sheeting and runs negative air machines with HEPA filters so spores do not drift into the rest of the home. Workers wear respirators and protective suits.

Porous materials with growth, such as drywall and carpet, are removed and bagged inside containment. Hard surfaces and framing are cleaned with HEPA vacuums and damp wiping, and surfaces are treated before the area is dried. The drying step uses the same readings-based approach as our structural drying work.

What Testing Can and Cannot Tell You

The Illinois Department of Public Health does not license individuals for mold testing or assessments, does not recommend testing as a first step and notes that no standards exist for deciding what amount of mold is acceptable. Testing can document what is present, but it does not set a pass-fail line.

For that reason we focus on visible growth, moisture readings and the water history. If you want testing for a lender, a landlord or a legal reason, an independent assessor can do it.

Questions From Lenders and Buyers

Buyers and lenders sometimes ask for a mold clearance letter. The Illinois Department of Public Health has no state standard for clearance, so what a lender accepts is set by the lender, and independent assessors can provide the testing they ask for.

We provide written scope, photographs and moisture readings that show the work done. If a sale is on the line, ask your agent or lender what they need before the work starts, so the paperwork matches the request.

Insurance and Mold

Many policies cover mold only when it follows a covered water loss, and some exclude it or cap it. Mold from long-term leaks or neglected humidity is often excluded. Your insurer decides coverage.

Document the growth with photos, record the date you found it and tell your insurer about the original water event. We provide a written scope you can share.

Mold Rules and Conditions in Illinois and North Chicago

The Mold Remediation Registration Act, Public Act 103-0893, became law in Illinois, but its requirements do not take effect until the state adopts its rules. The Department of Public Health published proposed rules in October 2025, so check with the department for the current status.

Lake County averages about 36 inches of precipitation a year, and basements near Lake Michigan face humid summers and wet springs. We serve North Chicago, Winthrop Harbor and Fort Sheridan.
